MeetingWise

Teaching new managers when not to call a meeting. A scenario-based e-learning course that moves newly promoted managers from simple rules ("No meeting without an agenda") to real judgement ("It depends").

This project started as a needs analysis in Harvard's learning design course. I then designed, built and tested the full course independently.

The Problem

Interviews with three employees at a Boston-area pharmaceutical company surfaced the same complaint: too many meetings, too little done in them, and not enough time left for real work. A follow-up interview pointed to two root causes: no meeting standards were enforced, and managers didn't see the problem.

So I designed for the other side of the table: newly promoted managers, the people best placed to change how meetings run.

Learner Persona

Promoted new managers in a pharmaceutical company, mainly with backgrounds in medical or science fields and moderate social skills. Technology-fluent and fast learners.

Design at a Glance

The course follows a meeting's life cycle: decide whether to meet, plan it, run it. A certification case at the end asks learners to apply all three.

Learning objectives. By the end of the course, managers will be able to:

  • Identify the prerequisites for a meeting: the right issue, a clear agenda, and the right people.
  • Design a high-impact meeting plan using attendee feedback and an effective agenda.
  • Facilitate focused meetings, adapting the agenda when the meeting's purpose requires it.

With no SME available, I built the content from published research, including McKinsey's meeting-roles model and studies on meeting fatigue, using AI to speed up the search.

Design Decisions

1. Scenarios that teach judgement, not just rules

What learners see: Module 1 opens with "Should we meet for this?", and the answer is No three times. Module 3 turns that around. Dr. Alex is 25 minutes into a 40-minute meeting, still on item 1. Should she power through? The answer: it depends. Two valid approaches follow, each with its own interaction and rationale: drop the tangent to the Issue List, or deliberately pivot the agenda.

Why it matters: Most corporate e-learning stops at rules. This scenario asks learners to weigh a trade-off against the meeting's purpose, the evaluate level of Bloom's Taxonomy, and keeps them from applying "no agenda, no meeting" rigidly. The scenarios are based on a real situation an employee described in my interviews.

Rules: the answer is No

Judgement: it depends

How I built it
  • In the yes/no scenarios, each answer button jumps to its own feedback slide.
  • On feedback slides, the back arrow jumps directly to the question slide instead of using "previous slide," so a learner returning from feedback lands on the question, not back on the feedback.
  • In the Dr. Alex scenario, both answers lead to the same "It depends" slide, which walks learners through both approaches in turn.

2. Built for adult learners

What learners see: Learners type their name on the first screen and are greeted with "Big Congrats on your promotion!" Their name returns in the certification scenario and on their certificate.

Why it matters: Adults engage when learning connects to their role and identity (Knowles). From the first screen, this is training for their new job.

How I built it
  • Added a text-entry field and stored the entry in a variable.
  • Referenced the variable wherever the learner's name appears.
  • Passed the same variable to a JavaScript trigger that uses the jsPDF library to generate a personalized PDF certificate.

3. Short, structured, easy to follow

What learners see: Three short modules, each ending with a one-screen summary. A course menu shows progress, with later modules locked until earlier ones are done.

Why it matters: Short modules reduce cognitive load (Sweller). Summaries consolidate key points before each quiz. Locking keeps the sequence intact, because each module builds on the last.

4. Practice that matches the skill

What learners see: Each interaction fits the thinking it practises: sorting issues into "meet / don't meet" (classify), matching roles to team members (apply), ranking feedback methods on a podium (evaluate), and dragging an off-track item into the agenda's Issue List (rehearse the real move). Ungraded quizzes close each module; the final certification requires 100%, with retry and review.

Why it matters: Practice that mirrors the real task transfers better than recall questions. Mastery-based certification means a passed learner has applied every module to a new case.

How I built it
  • Built custom correct and incorrect drop states for each drag-and-drop target.
  • On the agenda template, Next unlocks only after every hotspot has been opened.
  • Scripted a blinking animation with JavaScript to draw attention to a key element.
  • Set the certification quiz to 100% passing, with Retry and Review enabled.

Development

Before building in Storyline, I storyboarded each slide: the on-screen text, the programming and animation notes, and the graphics.

Visual Identity & Graphics

The visual language for Meeting Wise was designed to feel professional but approachable — suited to managers in a science-driven environment who are new to people-leadership. Clean layouts, purposeful color, and consistent iconography signal credibility without overwhelming the learner.

Evaluation

Currently piloting with companies in the Boston area. I plan to evaluate with the Kirkpatrick model:

LevelWhat I'd measure
1 · ReactionSurvey on relevance and satisfaction; xAPI data on completion, time spent, and navigation.
2 · LearningPractice quiz and certification scores.
3 · Behavior30–60 days later: number of meetings held, share of meetings with an agenda sent in advance, and use of feedback debriefs.
4 · ResultsHours recovered from meetings, project delivery speed, and employee satisfaction with meetings.

What I'd Do Next

Add the agenda template as a downloadable job aid. Test the course with newly promoted managers and revise the scenarios based on their feedback.
