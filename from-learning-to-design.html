From Learning Science to Design Practice

Theories of learning are most useful to me when they sharpen design choices. I use them less as labels and more as lenses for decisions like: How much complexity is appropriate here? Where might learners get stuck? What kinds of experiences will actually shift practice?

A few guiding principles in my practice

Structure as cognitive support

I break complex ideas into coherent segments, not just for clarity, but to respect the limits of attention and working memory. Transitions between segments are intentional: I signal what is changing, invite learners to recall what came before, and show how the pieces fit together.

Practice that is intellectually honest

I design practice tasks that resemble the kinds of decisions and trade-offs learners will face outside the learning environment. Rather than isolated trivia questions, I prefer tasks that require interpretation, prioritization, and judgment.

Activation of prior knowledge

Before introducing new material, I prompt learners to surface existing assumptions, experiences, or strategies. This makes it easier to connect, contrast, or revise what they already believe, rather than asking them to "add" information on top.

Deliberate moments of reflection

I build in short, structured pauses — not as filler, but as opportunities for learners to consolidate: "What just happened? What surprised me? How would I explain this to someone else?" These small interventions significantly change what is remembered and how it is used later.

Attention to the emotional texture of learning

Confusion, frustration, and uncertainty are common in meaningful learning. I try to design experiences that normalize these states and provide just enough structure and feedback that they become productive rather than paralyzing.
