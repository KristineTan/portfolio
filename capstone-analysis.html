Designing for Transfer: An Analysis of the LEAP Capstone

How ADDIE, backward design, UDL, and learning theory shaped Module 3 of the "AI and Productive Struggle" Canvas course — and what designing for faculty taught me about designing for anyone.

My capstone group project was the development of a Canvas module, "AI and Productive Struggle," created in partnership with the LEAP Initiative. Through an audit of LEAP's existing offerings and participant feedback, as well as consultations with Andres and Allison, we clarified that the central goal of the project was to help faculty become more open to experimenting with artificial intelligence in their teaching.

My primary responsibility was designing and developing Module 3: "AI Tools Gallery Walk." In this module, I searched for, evaluated, and explained AI tools — friction bot, AI oral assessment platforms, and cognitive partnership reviewer — that faculty could apply directly in their teaching.

Learners' Role in Informing Our Design

Learners played a central role in shaping our design decisions. One of our most valuable resources was Allison's "listening tour," a collection of interviews with HGSE faculty about how they were using generative AI to support teaching and student learning. These conversations revealed that faculty occupied a wide spectrum of attitudes toward AI, ranging from reluctant skeptics to enthusiastic super users. To better understand this diversity of perspectives, we intentionally interviewed three faculty members positioned at different points along this continuum.

The module's guiding theme — productive struggle and AI — emerged directly from these interviews. Faculty from different parts of the spectrum all expressed both concern and curiosity about AI's impact on student learning. Some worried that AI might eliminate the cognitive challenges essential for deep learning, while others were eager to explore how AI could scaffold students' thinking and create new forms of productive struggle. These contrasting perspectives helped us frame the module around a central pedagogical question: How can educators use AI to preserve and even enhance the kinds of struggle that promote meaningful learning?

Integrating ADDIE and Backward Design

During this analysis process, we identified a clear need for an asynchronous Canvas module that could serve faculty members who were interested in LEAP programming but were unable to attend live sessions because of scheduling constraints.

We chose Canvas because it is the learning management system most commonly used by our target audience. Designing within a familiar platform reduced unnecessary cognitive and logistical burdens, such as creating new accounts or learning an unfamiliar interface. By lowering these barriers to entry, we hoped to increase participation and improve the onboarding experience.

In developing this module, we drew on both the ADDIE Model and Backward Design frameworks. Backward design helped us maintain alignment between our learning objectives, assessments, and instructional activities, while ADDIE provided a structured process for analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation.

A Pivot in Design

My initial idea was to build the module around a detailed tutorial on creating custom chatbots. However, after consulting with Andres and learning that faculty already had access to those technical resources, I shifted my focus from a single tool to a broader ecosystem of AI tools — an approach more consistent with UDL. This change allowed us to present multiple entry points so faculty could engage with AI in ways that best matched their interests and teaching contexts.

At the same time, this project taught me that instructional design is rarely a linear process. What matters most is maintaining a coherent throughline that connects learner needs, design decisions, and intended outcomes.

Applying Universal Design for Learning

The design of Module 3 was grounded in Universal Design for Learning by intentionally reducing barriers and providing flexible ways for faculty to access, engage with, and respond to the content.

First, in terms of representation, I offered materials in multiple formats, including videos with transcripts, images with alt text, and written articles, so that faculty could access key ideas through more than one mode. Second, in terms of engagement, I embedded reflection quizzes throughout the module to prompt faculty to monitor their reactions, consider their openness to different AI tools, and connect the material to their own teaching contexts. Third, the module also supported action, expression, and social learning through discussion prompts that invited faculty to draft implementation plans, raise concerns, and respond to peers. Rather than treating accessibility as an afterthought, I was intentionally making these choices from the start to build flexibility that supports a wider range of learners with different levels of AI familiarity.

Cognitivist and Constructivist Principles

From a cognitivist perspective, I designed Module 3 to help faculty integrate new ideas about AI into their existing teaching knowledge structures. To do this, I organized each AI tool around familiar instructional contexts such as program design in HDE, TLL, and ELOE; policy analysis in EPA; and product prototyping in LDIT. By anchoring unfamiliar tools in recognizable teaching activities, the module activated prior knowledge and reduced the cognitive effort required to interpret new information, allowing faculty to focus on how AI might meaningfully extend their instructional practice.

For example, I used the "Friction Bot" to illustrate how AI could be embedded in authentic assignments to surface and support productive struggle. These examples were presented as interactive flip cards, which allowed faculty to selectively engage with the contexts most relevant to their teaching and control the order and pace of exploration. This structure supported focused attention and made it easier for learners to build connections between AI tools and their existing teaching schemas.

Constructivist principles were incorporated through the discussion board, where faculty were asked to articulate concrete adoption plans for integrating AI into their courses. By designing and sharing these plans, learners actively constructed their own understanding of how AI could enhance teaching. I also invited faculty who were hesitant or skeptical about AI to voice their concerns in the same discussion space. This created opportunities for participants to learn from one another, identify suitable teaching activities for AI augmentation, and reflect collectively on where AI might introduce new and meaningful forms of productive struggle.

Looking Ahead: Implementation and Evaluation

The aspect of this project I am most eager to pursue is implementation and evaluation. While our team developed a theoretically grounded and carefully designed module, its ultimate success depends on how faculty actually experience and use it. I would like to pilot the module with real learners and collect feedback to test the assumptions underlying our design. This process would allow me to validate what worked, identify where our assumptions were inaccurate, and make more informed instructional decisions in future iterations.

Coming Full Circle

This project deepened my understanding that instructional design is both systematic and adaptive. Frameworks such as ADDIE, Backward Design, UDL, and learning theories provided essential guidance, but meaningful design emerged through continuous iteration, stakeholder feedback, and a willingness to revise assumptions.
