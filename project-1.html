Meet the Learners Where They Are

This chapter focuses on the theory of learning and knowing, distinguishing the difference between experts and novice learners, defining learning with established theoretical frameworks, and exploring the essential questions to provoke thinking.

Experts and Novice Learners

Bridging my experience in Student Engagement with my work in Instructional Design, a central theme has emerged: "meeting the students where they are". This principle suggests that effective support is impossible without first diagnosing a learner's specific needs. In practice, this requires a deep understanding of their learners' background knowledge, learning styles. Specifically in this chapter, we focused the cognitive gap between experts and novices. Domain-specifically speaking, the more one learns, the more efficient the learning process becomes; experts can effortlessly weave new information into their existing networks of experience and participation. Because experts rely on these established patterns and mental models, it is easy for them to forget how difficult the material is for a beginner. Therefore, an instructional designer must remain acutely aware of this "curse of knowledge" to effectively scaffold materials that truly align with a novice learner's starting point.

Thanks to my professor, Bill Wisser, I have been able to integrate constructivism into my future design work to help learners at various levels. I am particularly drawn to social constructivism, where students work together to enable their collective learning. In this model, it is highly effective to pair a more advanced peer with a novice. The advanced peer benefits from the act of recalling and reframing the material to meet the novice's needs, while the novice benefits from the perspective of someone who has already begun making mental connections but is not yet an unreachable expert.

Solidifying My Understanding of Learning Theories

Behaviorism

This theory emphasizes external, observable behaviors, using rewards and punishment for reinforcement. In my work, behaviorism serves as a primary motivator: tokens, badges, and "congratulations" fireworks that fill the screen when a user finishes a module. Using these features reinforces the connection to the material and helps learners stay engaged in their journey, encouraging them to keep making progress.

Cognitivism

This approach engages learners by focusing on how they internally organize information into existing knowledge schemas. In my work, well-designed scaffolded practices—such as worksheets and discussion posts—rely on this mechanism. These opportunities provide space for learners to reflect on their existing knowledge and make distinctions between new skills and what they already know. Another example is the intentional pause; in online modules, these pauses are effective in helping learners reflect on their knowledge, thereby improving long-term retention.

Constructivism

Constructivism consists of two branches that highlight the different ways learners build their knowledge: social constructivism and cognitive constructivism. In my work, constructivist activities emphasize that learners must articulate their reasoning rather than simply providing the correct answers. I create opportunities for meaningful dialogue—through discussion prompts, peer feedback, or collaborative tasks—where diverse perspectives can clash, align, and evolve.

My experience tutoring teenagers further solidified these concepts through an experiential learning program I developed. By combining real-world tasks with guided reflection, this program roots itself in both cognitive and social constructivism. In these real working environments, students are not just receiving information; they are actively constructing their own understanding and learning experience through direct participation.

Essential Questions That Provoke Thinking

In this chapter we also explored what essential questions are and how to ask them. Central to this exploration is the use of essential questions, which carry four distinct connotations in the design of effective learning. First, they are important questions that recur throughout our lives, touching on timeless human experiences. Within a specific field of study, "essential" refers to the core ideas and inquiries that define the discipline itself. From a practical design perspective, these questions identify what is strictly needed for learning core content, ensuring that students grasp foundational concepts before moving to complex details. Finally, "essential" refers to questions that will most engage a specific and diverse set of learners, making the material personally relevant and accessible to everyone. By anchoring a curriculum in these four pillars, we can provoke deep thinking and help students organize new information into meaningful, enduring schemas.

Key Insight

Deliberately and strategically focusing on "bigger" questions helps learners engage with course content on a much deeper level. Overarching questions—those that cannot be answered in a few words and often lack a single "right" answer—probe the very essence of a subject. The intent is to evoke a process where learners construct meaning as they think through the material. We can facilitate this by analyzing key concepts, purpose, value, and the strategies or tactics surrounding contextualization. This approach is particularly vital in adult learning settings, where the contexts for knowledge transfer are often dynamic and complex. If instructional designers introduce these broad, essential terms at the outset, the experience becomes significantly more beneficial, as it directly aids in the transfer of knowledge and skills.
