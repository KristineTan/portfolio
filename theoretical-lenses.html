Theoretical Lenses I Use

I draw on multiple traditions in learning science. I rarely apply them in a pure form; instead, I let them inform different parts of the design process.

Behaviorist Influences: Clarity and Contingencies

From behaviorist traditions, I borrow the insistence on clarity: What, precisely, should change as a result of this learning experience?

In practice, this shows up as:

  • Well-specified performance outcomes, rooted in observable behaviors
  • Transparent criteria for success
  • Immediate, contingent feedback after key actions or decisions
  • I sometimes use progress indicators, levels, or badges — not as superficial gamification, but as visible traces of effort and improvement that help sustain engagement over time.

Cognitive Perspectives: Representation and Processing

Cognitive approaches direct my attention to how information is represented, processed, and stored.

I apply this by:

  • Designing materials that reduce unnecessary cognitive load (for example, aligning visual and verbal information, avoiding competing signals on the screen)
  • Sequencing tasks so that learners move from guided examples to more open-ended applications
  • Using comparisons, analogies, and counterexamples to deepen understanding of core ideas
  • Intentionally revisiting key concepts in varied contexts to strengthen retrieval and transfer
  • Here, I am less interested in naming specific effects and more interested in whether the design invites the kind of mental work that leads to robust understanding.

Constructivist and Experiential Traditions: Learning as Sense-Making

Constructivist and experiential theories remind me that learners actively construct meaning; they do not simply receive it.

This shapes my preference for:

  • Scenarios and simulations that approximate real constraints and ambiguity
  • Activities where learners must articulate their reasoning, not only their answers
  • Opportunities for dialogue — through discussion prompts, peer feedback, or collaborative tasks — where perspectives can clash, align, and evolve
  • Post-activity reflection that asks, "What did you notice about how you approached this? What might you try differently next time?"
  • Earlier in my work with teenagers, I saw how powerful it was to place learners in environments where the stakes felt real: projects tied to authentic problems, with visible consequences and a genuine sense of accomplishment. I carry that orientation into my current projects, even when the format is an online module or a blended program.

What This Means for the Experiences I Design

Bringing developmental psychology and learning science into design does not mean making courses heavier or more theoretical. It means being more precise and intentional about how people change.

If you look at my work, you will see:

  • Learning goals that are framed in terms of meaningful shifts in practice, not simply coverage of topics
  • Experiences that balance structure with openness, so learners are supported but not scripted
  • Assessments that function both as measurement and as learning opportunities in their own right
  • A through-line from initial engagement to long-term application, with deliberate attention to what helps learners remember, adapt, and reuse what they have learned

My aim is to build learning experiences that are intellectually honest, psychologically realistic, and practically useful — grounded in theory, but judged by whether they help real people do their work, and live their lives, differently.
