Design 🧩

On backward design, ADDIE, equity, assessment, and the cognitive apprenticeship — and what it means when frameworks start coming together.

Backward Design

Backward design has increasingly informed how I approach decision-making in my daily life. I often begin with the end goal and then work backward to determine the tasks, credentials, and milestones needed to reach that goal. We also adopted this logic as the guiding throughline for our project, LEAP.

For LEAP, our ultimate goal is to develop a module that encourages faculty to adopt AI and explore its potential to create productive struggle for learners. After clarifying this outcome, we integrated ADDIE with backward design and began developing our learner personas. When consulting with Alison, we tried to gather as much information as possible about our audience — their motivations, barriers, and prior knowledge. This process ensured that our design decisions remained aligned with our goal and allowed us to create materials that truly meet faculty needs and help them achieve the intended outcomes. At the same time, Alison emphasized that iteration and piloting could be especially valuable given that our topic — AI in education — is still relatively new and evolving quickly. This reminded us that while backward design helps maintain a clear alignment with the end goal, the design process itself cannot be entirely linear or perfectly planned.

In this sense, backward design helps balance the more modelized and structured process of ADDIE by continually reminding us to align our efforts with the intended outcome. However, I also realized that it is impossible to conduct a perfect analysis or follow a fixed cycle in practice. Instead, effective design often involves going back and forth with different stages in one framework and even selectively using components from different frameworks, as long as there is a clear throughline guiding the work.

Design Principles

My identity as a learning designer expanded in a meaningful way when I began learning about designing for equity and accessibility. Even I felt heavy reading the history of inequity and inequality brought by previous designs, I felt very hopeful because "design-for-all" is actually very actionable: Simply installing the WAVE Chrome extension has helped me identify accessibility issues on my personal website. Adding alt text to images and ensuring that materials across different modalities align with the content are small steps, but they make a difference. These practices encouraged me to start taking immediate action to design more intentionally for all users.

Additionally, I totally transformed my idea in formative assessment. I love the idea that everything we do around learning should ultimately serve learning itself. Growing up in an educational culture where exams and quizzes often felt like the endpoint of learning, this perspective was particularly inspiring. It motivates me to emphasize lifelong learning as a core value in my own design philosophy, where assessment and activities function as tools that support growth rather than final judgments of ability.

Lastly, the article on cognitive apprenticeship by Collins and Kapur resonated deeply with me in relation to deeper learning. The authors highlight how identity formation lies at the core of meaningful learning within communities of practice. This perspective aligns strongly with how I understand human development: people aspire to become someone, and that aspiration motivates them to achieve, explore, and learn. Beyond motivation, the article also discusses the importance of sequencing the global structure of learning before moving into more specific tasks. This idea connects closely with Wiggins and McTighe's concept of essential questions as "doorways to understanding," which guide learners toward transferable knowledge and the ability to solve authentic, complex problems.

This perspective pushes me, as a learning designer, to think beyond simply achieving the goals of a single module. Instead, it encourages me to situate learning within the broader subject or field and to adopt an expert perspective when designing experiences. By doing so, I can better probe learners' thinking, challenge them appropriately, and create opportunities for deeper engagement and more meaningful learning.

Coming Full Circle

It feels like everything from HPL to PCE to TLL has been coming into a full circle.
